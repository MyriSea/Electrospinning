How electrospinning works

Electrospinning uses a high electric field to pull a polymer solution into fibres thousands of times thinner than a human hair. The result is a non‑woven mat of nano‑ to micro‑scale fibres with a huge surface area.

The setup

Syringe pump

Pushes polymer solution through a metal needle at a slow, steady flow rate (typically around 0.1–2 mL/h).

High‑voltage supply

Connects to the needle and charges the solution, usually at 10–30 kV.

Grounded collector

A flat plate or rotating drum a few centimetres to 20 cm away. It completes the electric field and catches the fibres.

What happens, step by step

  1. Charge builds up

    Voltage makes charges accumulate at the surface of the droplet hanging from the needle. Like charges repel, and the field pulls the droplet toward the collector.

  2. The Taylor cone forms

    Electrostatic stress stretches the droplet against surface tension. Above a critical voltage, it becomes a cone with a half‑angle of about 49.3°, known as the Taylor cone.

  3. A jet is ejected

    Once the electrostatic force beats surface tension, a thin charged jet leaves the cone tip and travels in a straight line for a short distance.

  4. Whipping instability

    Repulsion between charges along the jet makes it bend and loop in a growing spiral. The path becomes much longer than the gap, so the jet stretches by a factor of thousands.

  5. The solvent evaporates

    While the jet whips, solvent leaves and the polymer solidifies. The diameter shrinks into the nanometre to micrometre range.

  6. Fibres land on the collector

    Dry fibres deposit randomly into a non‑woven mat. A rotating collector can align them instead.

Why it matters

Electrospun mats are used in tissue engineering scaffolds, wound dressings, drug delivery, filtration membranes, battery separators and sensors. The fibres resemble the fibrous extracellular matrix, which is why biomaterials researchers use them so often.
